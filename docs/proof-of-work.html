Proof of Work: price forecasting for Assam farmers

NESFIC 2026 · Challenge NESFIC-D-35, Agricultural Commodity Price Intelligence and Forecasting for Farmer Decisions · Team from IIT Roorkee · Generated 25 Nov 2026

1. What this document shows

A working prototype that forecasts wholesale potato prices in Assam 1, 2 and 4 weeks ahead from real public market data, tested against what actually happened, and turned into a plain hold-or-sell message for a farmer. Potato was chosen because it can be stored, so the hold-or-sell decision is real.

What is real: every number, table and chart in sections 2 to 6 was produced by the code listed in section 9 from public Agmarknet price records. No figure was typed in by hand. What is not real yet: the three screens in section 7 are design mock-ups, and nothing here uses data from the Department, the AMIU of Assam Agricultural University or any farmer. Limits are listed in section 8.

Headline result, stated plainly: across 4 Assam districts and 226 test forecasts, the prototype's 1-week error is 6.2% against 6.1% for simply repeating the last known price. It ties that baseline; it does not yet beat it. The more useful findings are how incomplete the price record is (Figure 1), how weakly the Assam market tracks its nearest large market week to week (section 3), and a price calendar built on 18 years of data (section 6).

3. Why a comparison market helps

Siliguri, in north Bengal, is a large wholesale market on the road and rail corridor into the North East. Its recent price history is denser than that of the Assam market, so we tested whether it can carry information for the weeks when Assam reports nothing.

Week-on-week price changes in the two markets have a correlation of 0.245 (346 weeks where both reported; strongest when Siliguri is shifted by 0 weeks, where a positive shift means Siliguri moves first). That is a weak link. Week to week, the Assam price moves largely on its own, which is one reason the borrowed signal adds little in section 5. Testing other source markets (for example Kolkata, Burdwan and Agra) and adding arrival quantities is the first job of the funded work.

4. Method

Daily records are grouped into a weekly median price per market. A record is dropped when it is more than 4 times above or below the local price level, which catches prices typed per kilogram. For a forecast made at the end of week t for week t + h:

  • The starting point is the last Assam price, if it is at most 4 weeks old. If it is older, the system says "no recent price" and gives no forecast.
  • Five signals adjust it: how far Siliguri has moved since Assam last reported; how far the Assam price sits from its usual gap to Siliguri, where "usual" means its last 26 reports within two years; Siliguri's 4-week trend; the usual seasonal move between the two dates; and the Assam market's own recent trend.
  • A ridge regression, one per horizon, learns the weights from past weeks only. How strongly it is pulled towards "no change" is chosen on the newest fifth of those past weeks. The range comes from the 10th and 90th percentile of its 150 most recent errors.

Three simple methods are run alongside as yardsticks: the last known price; the price in the same week last year; and the last known price moved by Siliguri's change since then.

Plain reading: at 1 week ahead the model's error was 0.3% higher than the last-known-price baseline, so the model did not beat it. At 2 weeks it was 0.1% lower than the last-known-price baseline. At 4 weeks it was 4.4% higher than the last-known-price baseline, so the model did not beat it. In this market the prototype ties or trails the simplest possible method at every horizon. We report that as it is. It sets the measured starting line that the funded work has to beat. The stated range held 74% to 78% of the time, below the 80% intended, so it is too narrow and needs recalibrating.

One revision was made, and both results are shown. The first version measured the Assam price against its all-time average gap to Siliguri and used a fixed regression strength. Measured on the records downloaded at that point, its error against the last known price changed by -1.6% at 1 week, -3.3% at 2 weeks, -8.7% at 4 weeks (negative means worse). The cause was visible in Figure 1: this market barely reported between 2020 and 2025, so the all-time gap was years out of date. The revision described in section 4 was made once, for that stated reason, and the table above is its result. No further tuning was done against the test weeks.

System outline

1. SourcesAMIU daily spot prices and arrivals; Agmarknet; comparable markets in other states
2. CleaningUnit and typing checks; weekly series; every gap flagged, never silently filled
3. ModelsShort-term forecast with borrowed signal; price calendar; accuracy tracker
4. AdviceHold, sell or no clear signal, with a range and a plain reason
5. ChannelsSMS and voice; extension app; dashboard; data feed for other systems
Later phasesArrival quantities, futures prices, weather and cold-storage stocks as extra signals
Feedback loopPrices reported from the field and forecast-against-actual results flow back into steps 2 and 3

8. Limits of this prototype

  • Accuracy: across the scored districts the model roughly ties the last-known-price method. It is a measured baseline, not a finished forecaster.
  • One crop. The method is not tied to potato, but it has only been tested on potato.
  • Public Agmarknet prices only. AMIU's own daily prices and arrival quantities, which the Department names, were not available to us.
  • The price is a weekly median across the markets of a district, so it hides differences between varieties and between markets.
  • The 5% hold-or-sell threshold is a placeholder. The real figure depends on storage cost and spoilage, which must come from the Department and farmers.
  • The price calendar describes past seasons. It is not a forecast of next year's prices.
  • No farmer or extension worker has used any of this yet.
